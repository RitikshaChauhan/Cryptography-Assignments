Q1.
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Define network security and explain its fundamental principles and objectives.
Discuss the importance of confidentiality, integrity, availability,
authentication, and authorization in securing network systems.

ANSWER:

Definition
Network security is the set of policies, practices, technologies and devices
used to protect the usability, reliability, integrity and safety of a computer
network and the data travelling through it. It aims to prevent unauthorized
access, misuse, modification, destruction or disclosure of network resources
and data. It combines hardware (firewalls, routers, IDS/IPS), software
(antivirus, VPN clients, patches), cryptography and administrative controls
(policies, training).

Fundamental principles and objectives
 1. Protect data in transit and at rest from eavesdropping and tampering.
 2. Allow only legitimate users and devices to use network resources.
 3. Keep the network and services available for authorized users.
 4. Detect, respond to and recover from attacks quickly.
 5. Apply defence in depth (multiple layers), least privilege (give only the
    minimum access needed), separation of duties and fail-safe defaults.

The CIA triad plus authentication and authorization

 1. Confidentiality - information is disclosed only to authorized parties.
    Importance: protects passwords, banking details, personal and business
    data from sniffing and leaks.
    Mechanisms: encryption (AES, TLS, IPsec VPN), access control, data
    classification.
    Example: HTTPS prevents someone on public Wi-Fi from reading a login page.

 2. Integrity - data is accurate and has not been altered by unauthorized
    persons during storage or transmission.
    Importance: a changed bank transfer amount or tampered software update can
    cause large damage.
    Mechanisms: hash functions (SHA-256), MAC/HMAC, digital signatures,
    checksums, version control.
    Example: a downloaded file's SHA-256 hash is compared with the published
    value.

 3. Availability - systems, services and data are accessible when needed by
    authorized users.
    Importance: downtime of banking, hospital or e-commerce services causes
    financial and safety loss.
    Mechanisms: redundancy, load balancing, backups, DDoS protection, UPS,
    patch management, disaster recovery plans.
    Example: a DDoS attack attacks availability.

 4. Authentication - verifying the claimed identity of a user, device or
    message source ("Who are you?").
    Factors: something you know (password, PIN), something you have (OTP
    token, smart card, phone), something you are (fingerprint, face).
    Mechanisms: passwords, multi-factor authentication (MFA), digital
    certificates, Kerberos, biometrics.
    Importance: stops impersonation and spoofing.

 5. Authorization - deciding what an authenticated user is allowed to do
    ("What can you do?"), i.e. permissions and privileges.
    Mechanisms: access control lists (ACL), role-based access control (RBAC),
    least privilege, firewalls rules, file permissions.
    Example: a student account can read course material but not change
    grades.
    Authentication always comes before authorization.

Related objectives: non-repudiation (sender cannot deny sending, via digital
signatures), accountability/auditing (logs), and privacy.

Diagram:
                      +-------------------+
                      |  Network Security |
                      +---------+---------+
          +----------+----------+----------+-----------+
          |          |          |          |           |
   Confidentiality Integrity Availability Authentication Authorization
   (secrecy)     (no change) (always up) (who are you?)  (what can you do?)

Q2.
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Explain the threat landscape in network security. Discuss different types of
network attacks and vulnerabilities, along with suitable examples and
preventive measures.

ANSWER:

Threat landscape
The threat landscape is the overall set of threats, threat actors,
vulnerabilities and attack methods that can affect networks. Threat actors
include script kiddies, hacktivists, cybercriminals, insiders (employees),
organised groups and state-sponsored attackers (Advanced Persistent Threats,
APT). Motives: money, espionage, disruption, revenge and ideology. The
landscape keeps changing with cloud, IoT, remote work, mobile devices and AI,
which enlarge the attack surface.

Types of attacks
 A. Passive attacks (do not alter data; hard to detect, so prevent with
    encryption): eavesdropping/sniffing (e.g. Wireshark on open Wi-Fi) and
    traffic analysis.
 B. Active attacks (modify data or disrupt service): masquerade, replay,
    modification, denial of service.

 Specific attacks, examples and preventive measures:

 1. Malware (virus, worm, trojan, ransomware, spyware, botnets)
    Example: WannaCry ransomware (2017) spread by an SMB vulnerability.
    Prevention: antivirus/EDR, patching, backups, email filtering, restricted
    privileges.

 2. Phishing and social engineering (spear phishing, vishing, pretexting)
    Example: fake bank e-mail asking for OTP.
    Prevention: user training, spam filters, MFA, DMARC/SPF/DKIM, verifying
    links.

 3. Denial of Service (DoS) / Distributed DoS (DDoS)
    Floods a server/link (SYN flood, UDP flood, DNS amplification) to make it
    unavailable. Example: Mirai botnet (2016).
    Prevention: rate limiting, firewalls, CDN/DDoS scrubbing services, load
    balancers, redundancy.

 4. Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) and session hijacking
    Attacker secretly relays and alters communication (rogue Wi-Fi, ARP
    poisoning, SSL stripping).
    Prevention: TLS with certificate validation, HSTS, VPN, secure cookies,
    dynamic ARP inspection.

 5. Spoofing: IP spoofing, ARP spoofing, DNS spoofing/cache poisoning, MAC
    spoofing, e-mail spoofing.
    Prevention: ingress/egress filtering, DNSSEC, port security, authentication
    of packets (IPsec).

 6. Sniffing / packet capture on unencrypted protocols (HTTP, FTP, Telnet).
    Prevention: use HTTPS, SSH, SFTP, VPN; switched networks.

 7. Password attacks: brute force, dictionary, credential stuffing, rainbow
    tables.
    Prevention: strong passwords, salted slow hashing, account lockout, MFA.

 8. Application-layer attacks: SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS),
    CSRF, buffer overflow, directory traversal.
    Prevention: input validation, prepared statements, WAF, secure coding,
    regular testing.

 9. Replay attack: a valid captured message is re-sent later.
    Prevention: timestamps, nonces, sequence numbers, session tokens.

10. Insider threats: employees misusing access.
    Prevention: least privilege, monitoring and logging, separation of duties.

11. Zero-day exploits and APTs: exploits of unknown vulnerabilities and long,
    stealthy intrusions.
    Prevention: defence in depth, network segmentation, threat intelligence,
    IDS/IPS, zero-trust approach.

12. Wireless attacks: rogue access points, evil twin, WEP/WPS cracking.
    Prevention: WPA3/WPA2-AES, disable WPS, wireless IDS.

Common vulnerabilities
 - Unpatched operating systems and applications.
 - Weak, default or reused passwords.
 - Misconfigured firewalls, open ports and services, cloud storage left
   public.
 - Unencrypted protocols and weak ciphers.
 - Lack of input validation in software (SQL injection, buffer overflow).
 - Poor physical security and lack of user awareness.
 - Unmanaged IoT/BYOD devices; third-party and supply-chain weaknesses.

General preventive measures
 Firewalls and network segmentation (DMZ, VLANs); IDS/IPS; VPN and encryption;
 timely patch management; vulnerability scanning and penetration testing;
 MFA and strong access control; logging/monitoring (SIEM); regular backups and
 incident-response plan; security awareness training; zero-trust model.

Q3.

Discuss the legal and ethical considerations in network security. Explain the
importance of data privacy, responsible security testing, authorized access,
and compliance with applicable cybersecurity laws.

ANSWER:

Network security professionals have powerful skills and access to sensitive
data, so their work is limited by laws (what they MUST or MUST NOT do) and by
ethics (what they SHOULD do).

1. Data privacy
   - Organizations collect personal data (names, Aadhaar/ID numbers, health and
     financial data); it must be collected lawfully, used only for the stated
     purpose, stored securely, kept only as long as needed, and shared only
     with consent or legal basis.
   - Principles: lawfulness and consent, purpose limitation, data
     minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, security, accountability.
   - Technical controls: encryption, access control, anonymisation/
     pseudonymisation, logging, breach notification.
   - Importance: prevents identity theft, protects individual rights and
     builds user trust; breaches lead to fines and loss of reputation.

2. Responsible (ethical) security testing
   - Penetration testing and vulnerability assessment are legal only when done
     with written permission (scope, targets, time window, rules of
     engagement, and a non-disclosure agreement).
   - Ethical hackers ("white hat") must stay within scope, avoid damaging or
     disrupting systems, avoid accessing or copying real personal data more
     than necessary, document findings and report them promptly and
     confidentially.
   - Responsible disclosure: report a found vulnerability to the owner/vendor
     privately, give time to fix it (e.g. 90 days), and do not publish exploit
     details before a patch. Bug bounty programmes provide legal channels.
   - "Black hat" (malicious) and "grey hat" (unauthorized, though well
     meaning) testing is illegal.

3. Authorized access
   - Accessing a computer, network or data without permission, or exceeding
     the permission given, is a crime in almost every country.
   - Authorization must be based on roles and need-to-know, with strong
     authentication, logs and audits; administrators must not misuse
     privileged access (e.g. reading employees' mail without legal basis).
   - Sniffing, scanning or cracking passwords on networks that you do not own
     or have no permission for is illegal, even without bad intention.

4. Compliance with cybersecurity laws and standards
   India:
    - Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended 2008): Section 43
      (unauthorized access, damage, data theft - compensation), Section 66
      (computer-related offences, imprisonment/fine), 66C (identity theft),
      66D (cheating by personation using computer), 66F (cyber terrorism),
      Section 67 (obscene content), Section 69 (government interception and
      monitoring powers), Section 72 (breach of confidentiality and privacy),
      and Section 43A/ reasonable security practices.
    - CERT-In Directions (2022): cyber incidents must be reported to CERT-In
      within 6 hours of noticing; logs must be kept for 180 days.
    - Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act): consent-based
      processing of digital personal data, duties of data fiduciaries, rights
      of data principals, penalties for breaches (very large fines).
   International:
    - GDPR (EU) - strict privacy and breach-notification rules with heavy fines
      (up to 4% of global turnover).
    - HIPAA (health data, USA), PCI DSS (payment card security standard),
      CFAA (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, USA), Budapest Convention on
      Cybercrime.
   Standards/frameworks: ISO/IEC 27001, NIST Cybersecurity Framework, SOC 2.
   Importance of compliance: avoids penalties and legal action, protects
   customers, enables international business, and gives a baseline of good
   practice. Organizations should perform audits and keep documented
   policies and incident response plans.

Ethical principles (e.g. ACM/(ISC)2 codes): act honestly and in public
interest, respect privacy and confidentiality, do no harm, obtain permission
before testing, avoid conflicts of interest, maintain competence, and report
vulnerabilities and incidents responsibly.

Explain the role of cryptography in securing network communications. Describe
the working and security features of SSL/TLS and IPsec protocols.

ANSWER:

Role of cryptography in network security
Cryptography turns insecure networks (like the Internet) into secure channels:
 - Confidentiality: symmetric encryption (AES, ChaCha20) hides data.
 - Integrity: hash functions and MACs (SHA-256, HMAC, AEAD modes like GCM)
   detect modification.
 - Authentication: digital signatures, certificates and challenge-response
   prove the identity of parties.
 - Key exchange: Diffie-Hellman/ECDH and RSA let strangers agree on keys.
 - Non-repudiation: digital signatures.
 - Protection against replay: nonces, sequence numbers, timestamps.
Network protocols apply these at different layers: TLS at the transport/
application layer, IPsec at the network layer, WPA2/3 at the link layer.

A. SSL/TLS (Secure Sockets Layer / Transport Layer Security)
   SSL was designed by Netscape (SSL 3.0, 1996) and standardised as TLS by the
   IETF (TLS 1.0 in 1999, 1.2 in 2008, 1.3 in 2018, RFC 8446). SSL and early
   TLS (1.0/1.1) are obsolete; use TLS 1.2 or TLS 1.3. TLS runs above TCP and
   protects application protocols such as HTTPS (port 443), SMTPS, IMAPS,
   FTPS, etc.

   Structure:
     Application (HTTP) -> [ TLS Handshake | Alert | Change Cipher Spec |
     Application Data ] -> TLS Record Protocol (fragmentation, MAC/AEAD,
     encryption) -> TCP -> IP

   Working (handshake, simplified TLS 1.2/1.3):
     1. ClientHello: client sends supported TLS versions, cipher suites, a
        random number (and, in TLS 1.3, its key share for ECDHE).
     2. ServerHello: server chooses version and cipher suite, sends its random
        value (and key share).
     3. Server authentication: server sends its X.509 certificate chain; the
        client validates it (trusted CA, validity period, domain name,
        revocation status) and the server proves possession of its private key
        (signature).
     4. Key exchange: a shared secret is created by ephemeral Diffie-Hellman
        (ECDHE), or in TLS 1.2 optionally by RSA key transport.
     5. Key derivation: both sides derive symmetric session keys from the
        shared secret and the random values (HKDF/PRF).
     6. Finished messages: each side sends a MAC of the whole handshake to
        confirm that nothing was tampered with.
     7. Application data: protected with the symmetric cipher and AEAD
        (e.g. AES-256-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305).

     Client                                         Server
       |--- ClientHello (ciphers, random, key share) -->|
       |<-- ServerHello, Certificate, signature, -------|
       |    key share, Finished                         |
       |--- Finished (keys derived on both sides) ----->|
       |<========= Encrypted application data =========>|

   Security features:
     - Server authentication (and optional client authentication) using
       certificates; protection against impersonation and MITM.
     - Confidentiality by symmetric encryption; integrity by MAC/AEAD.
     - Forward secrecy with ephemeral (EC)DHE keys: recorded traffic cannot be
       decrypted later even if the server's private key is stolen (always in
       TLS 1.3).
     - Protection against replay and tampering using sequence numbers and
       handshake verification; downgrade protection.
     - TLS 1.3 improvements: only 1 round trip (faster), removed weak
       algorithms (RC4, 3DES, SHA-1, static RSA, CBC), encrypted handshake
       messages, optional 0-RTT resumption.

B. IPsec (Internet Protocol Security)
   IPsec is a suite of protocols (RFC 4301 and others) that secures IP traffic
   at the network layer, so all applications are protected transparently. It
   is mainly used for VPNs (site-to-site and remote access). It provides
   confidentiality, integrity, data origin authentication, anti-replay
   protection and access control.

   Main components:
     1. Authentication Header (AH, protocol 51): integrity and authentication
        of the entire IP packet (including immutable header fields) and anti-
        replay; no encryption. Not compatible with NAT.
     2. Encapsulating Security Payload (ESP, protocol 50): encryption plus
        optional authentication/integrity and anti-replay of the payload
        (most commonly used; works with NAT using NAT-T on UDP 4500).
     3. Security Association (SA): a one-way logical agreement defining the
        algorithms, keys, lifetime and a Security Parameter Index (SPI);
        stored in the Security Association Database (SAD). The Security Policy
        Database (SPD) says which traffic is protected.
     4. Internet Key Exchange (IKEv1/IKEv2, UDP 500): authenticates the peers
        (pre-shared key or certificates), negotiates the SAs and exchanges keys
        using Diffie-Hellman.

   Modes of operation:
     - Transport mode: only the payload (data) of the IP packet is protected;
       original IP header is kept. Used for host-to-host communication.
     - Tunnel mode: the entire original IP packet is encrypted/authenticated
       and encapsulated in a new IP packet with a new header. Used for
       gateway-to-gateway VPNs, hiding the internal addresses.

        Tunnel mode ESP packet:
        [ New IP header | ESP header | Original IP header + Data | ESP trailer | ESP auth ]
                                      <------- encrypted ------->
                                      <----------- authenticated ----------->

   Working: (1) interesting traffic matches the policy; (2) IKE phase 1 sets
   up a secure, authenticated channel; (3) IKE phase 2 (child SA) negotiates
   IPsec SAs; (4) data is sent protected with ESP/AH; (5) SAs are re-keyed or
   deleted on expiry.

   Security features: strong encryption (AES), integrity (HMAC-SHA-2), peer
   authentication (certificates/PSK), perfect forward secrecy (DH), replay
   protection with sequence numbers, protection against IP spoofing and MITM.

Comparison:
   Feature        | TLS/SSL                    | IPsec
   ---------------+----------------------------+--------------------------------
   Layer          | Transport/application      | Network (IP) layer
   Protection of  | One application connection | All IP traffic between hosts/
                  | (per application)          | networks
   Typical use    | HTTPS, e-mail, web VPN     | Site-to-site and remote VPN
   Setup          | Easy, no client software   | More complex; client/gateway
                  | needed for browsers        | configuration
   Authentication | Mostly server (certificate)| Mutual (PSK or certificates)

What is Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)? Explain its components and working.
Discuss the role of digital certificates and Certificate Authorities in
establishing trust and securing network communications.

ANSWER:

Definition
Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is the framework of roles, policies, hardware,
software and procedures needed to create, manage, distribute, use, store and
revoke digital certificates and to manage public-key encryption. Its purpose
is to bind a public key to the identity of its owner in a trusted way, so that
users can be sure who owns a public key (solving the public-key authenticity
problem and stopping man-in-the-middle attacks).

Components of PKI
 1. Certificate Authority (CA): trusted third party that issues, signs, renews
    and revokes digital certificates. Types: root CA (offline, self-signed,
    most trusted), intermediate/subordinate CAs (issue end-entity
    certificates), and public vs private (enterprise) CAs.
 2. Registration Authority (RA): verifies the identity of the applicant on
    behalf of the CA before a certificate is issued.
 3. Digital certificate (X.509): an electronic document that binds a public
    key to an identity.
 4. Certificate repository/directory: storage where certificates and
    revocation information are published (LDAP, web).
 5. Certificate revocation mechanism: Certificate Revocation Lists (CRL) and
    Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP, including OCSP stapling) tell
    whether a certificate is still valid.
 6. End entities: users, servers, devices or applications that hold
    certificates and keys, and relying parties that verify them.
 7. Key management and secure storage: key generation, backup/escrow,
    archival, hardware security modules (HSM), smart cards, tokens.
 8. Policies and standards: Certificate Policy (CP), Certification Practice
    Statement (CPS), audits (e.g. WebTrust).
 (In India, CAs are licensed and regulated by the Controller of Certifying
 Authorities (CCA) under the IT Act, 2000.)

Digital certificate contents (X.509 v3)
   Version, serial number, signature algorithm, issuer name, validity (not
   before/not after), subject name, subject public key info, extensions
   (key usage, subject alternative name, basic constraints, CRL/OCSP
   locations) and the CA's digital signature over all these fields.

Working of PKI
  1. Key generation: the applicant (e.g. a web server) generates a public/
     private key pair; the private key never leaves the owner.
  2. Certificate Signing Request (CSR): the applicant sends the public key and
     identity information to the RA/CA.
  3. Identity verification: the RA/CA validates the domain/organization
     (DV, OV or EV validation).
  4. Certificate issuance: the CA signs the certificate with its private key
     and returns it; it is published in a repository.
  5. Use: during, for example, a TLS handshake the server sends its
     certificate; the client verifies:
        - the CA's signature using the CA's public key (follow the chain up to
          a trusted root stored in the browser/OS),
        - validity dates, domain name match and intended key usage,
        - that it has not been revoked (CRL/OCSP).
     Then the client trusts the server's public key and uses it for
     authentication and key exchange.
  6. Renewal and revocation: certificates expire (now at most about a year for
     public TLS) and are revoked if the private key is compromised.

  Chain of trust:
        Root CA (self-signed, in trust store)
              |  signs
        Intermediate CA
              |  signs
        End-entity certificate (www.example.com)

Role of digital certificates
  - Prove the identity of a server, person, device or software (authentication).
  - Distribute public keys safely for encryption and signature verification.
  - Enable HTTPS, secure e-mail (S/MIME), code signing, VPN authentication,
    document signing, smart card login and IoT device identity.
  - Provide non-repudiation, together with digital signatures.

Role of Certificate Authorities in trust
  - CAs act as trusted third parties: if you trust the CA, you can trust any
    certificate it signs. Browsers and operating systems ship with a list of
    trusted root CAs.
  - A CA must verify identities carefully, protect its private key, publish
    revocation status and follow audit rules. If a CA is compromised or
    issues false certificates (e.g. DigiNotar, 2011), trust is lost and the CA
    is removed from trust stores.
  - Other trust models: web of trust (PGP), and Certificate Transparency logs
    for public monitoring of issued certificates.

How PKI secures network communications
  - TLS/HTTPS: authenticates websites and establishes encrypted sessions.
  - IPsec/VPN and Wi-Fi (WPA-Enterprise/EAP-TLS): certificate-based
    authentication of gateways, users and devices.
  - E-mail and document security: S/MIME and digital signatures.
  - Software/firmware signing to ensure authenticity and integrity.
